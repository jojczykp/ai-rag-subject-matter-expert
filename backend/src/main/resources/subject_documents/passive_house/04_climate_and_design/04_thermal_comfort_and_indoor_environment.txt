TITLE: Thermal Comfort and Indoor Environmental Quality
TAGS: comfort, operative-temperature, humidity, acoustics, air-quality

Thermal comfort depends on more than air temperature. Operative temperature combines air temperature and mean radiant temperature. In a well-insulated building, warm interior surfaces reduce radiant asymmetry and allow comfort at moderate air temperatures.

Cold window surfaces can create downdraught and radiant discomfort even without measurable air leakage. High-performance glazing and suitable window placement reduce these effects. Warm floors and controlled air speeds also support comfort.

Relative humidity affects comfort, health and building durability. Very high humidity encourages mould and dust mites; very low humidity can cause dryness. Appropriate ranges depend on climate, season, occupancy and regulations. Control moisture sources and provide ventilation rather than relying on arbitrary humidification.

Indoor air quality requires source control, filtration and ventilation. Choose low-emitting materials and protect ducts during construction. Isolate garages, combustion equipment, refuse rooms and chemical storage. Avoid unvented combustion appliances.

Acoustic comfort is integral. Low-energy buildings can be very quiet externally, making mechanical noise more noticeable. Size ducts for low velocity, use attenuators where needed, isolate vibration and verify bedroom noise at normal operation.

Outdoor noise may make window-based summer ventilation impractical. This should influence cooling and ventilation design. Similarly, poor outdoor air quality may require better filtration and controlled intake placement.

Occupant control improves satisfaction when controls are understandable and effective. Provide operable windows where appropriate, local temperature adjustment within a reasonable range and clear shading controls. Automatic systems should not make occupants feel trapped.
